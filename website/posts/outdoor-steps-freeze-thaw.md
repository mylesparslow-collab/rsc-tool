Concrete steps and wood steps break down differently, but around Saratoga County the cause is almost always water that gets in and then freezes. Concrete cracks, flakes and heaves. Wood rots at the bottom of the stringers and the treads work loose. Small cracks and a flaking surface can be fixed, but steps that tilt, pull away from the house or shift every winter need to be rebuilt on real footings. You use entry steps every day in snow and ice, so a loose or uneven one is a real fall hazard.

## What a winter does to a set of steps

Water gets bigger when it freezes. Let it into a crack in a stoop or the end grain at the foot of a stringer, and every freeze pries the gap a little wider. Our winters cross the freezing line over and over between November and April. A hairline crack you notice at Thanksgiving can be a loose chunk of nosing by March.

The ground moves too. Wet soil swells as it freezes and lifts whatever is sitting on it. That's frost heave. Steps poured on a slab or set on shallow footings rise in January and come back down unevenly in April. Give it a few years and the steps pull away from the house, tip forward, and crack.

Salt makes concrete worse. De-icers keep the surface cycling between frozen and thawed, and the top layer pops off in flakes.

## Reading a concrete stoop

Check your own steps against this.

| What you see | What's going on |
|---|---|
| Hairline cracks, steps still level | Shrinkage or minor movement. Seal them to keep water out |
| Flaking or pitted surface (spalling) | Freeze-thaw and salt eating the top layer |
| Broken corners or tread edges | Impact plus freeze-thaw. A trip hazard |
| A gap growing between the steps and the house | The steps are settling or heaving on their own, apart from the foundation |
| Steps tilted forward or sideways | Frost heave or settling under the base |
| Wide cracks that get bigger every year | The whole thing is moving. A patch won't hold |

If the steps are level and still tight to the house, you've got repair options. Narrow cracks take a flexible exterior concrete crack sealant. Broken nosings and corners can be built back up with a bonded repair mortar. A flaking surface can get a thin bonded overlay, as long as the concrete under it is sound. After that, a penetrating sealer on clean, dry concrete slows down how much water and salt it soaks up.

None of that works on steps that move. If the base heaves, the patch cracks right along with it. Steps that have tilted, settled or separated from the house need new support. That can be new concrete steps on footings below frost depth, or tearing out the stoop and putting a wood or composite stair on proper footings in its place.

## Checking wood steps

Wood entry stairs and deck stairs fail in their own order. The bottom of each stringer goes first, especially where it sits on dirt, a paver, or a concrete pad that holds water. Treads cup and loosen as the fasteners back out through a year of swelling and shrinking. The ends of the treads and the wood around each screw go soft, because that's where water sits. Posts and footings heave, so the stair goes out of level or the landing tilts. And the handrail starts to wobble once its posts loosen.

Take a screwdriver to the bottom few inches of every stringer and the base of every post. Soft wood there is the warning that counts. A bad tread is an easy swap and a stringer can sometimes be repaired, but rotted stringers on an older stair are our cue to rebuild it.

When we build it back, the stair sits on a concrete pad or footing so the wood stays up off the ground. The framing is treated or rot-resistant, and there's enough gap between treads for water to drain through. We like composite treads and risers over treated framing for entry steps. Composite doesn't rot and it takes a snow shovel better than wood does. Our post on [composite vs wood decking in upstate NY](/blog/composite-vs-wood-decking-upstate-ny/) goes through the tradeoffs.

## Footings and permits

Steps that move every winter need footings that go down below the frost line. Frost depth is set locally, so call your town or city building department to find out the number for your address and whether the job needs a permit. Steps and landings attached to the house often do, especially when the work changes the structure of the entry.

## Rails, ice and the rest of the winter

The New York State Residential Code calls for a handrail on stairs with four or more risers, mounted 34 to 38 inches above the tread nosings. We'd put a solid rail on a two- or three-step entry too. In February it's the thing everybody grabs. Our post on [when stairs need a handrail](/blog/when-do-stairs-need-a-handrail/) has the details.

A lot of winter falls start with a downspout. If yours dumps onto the steps or right beside them, that water refreezes into a sheet every night, so move it. On concrete, use a de-icer labeled safe for concrete, or sand for grip, and keep salt off new concrete entirely for at least its first winter. Shovel wood and composite treads with a plastic blade so you don't gouge them. Light the steps all the way to the bottom one.

Fix loose treads and broken nosings in the fall, before the first snow covers them. It belongs on your [fall home maintenance checklist](/blog/fall-home-checklist-saratoga/) next to the gutters.

If someone in the house is starting to struggle with steps, plan for that now. A lower rise per step, a wider landing, or a ramp might be the better fix. Our post on [ramps and safer entry steps](/blog/ramps-and-safer-entry-steps/) walks through those choices.

## Patch or rebuild

Patch when the steps are level, solid and still tight to the house, and the damage is on the surface or limited to a part or two. Rebuild when they've heaved, tilted or pulled away, when the cracks grow every year, or when the stringers and posts are soft. Look right after the thaw, when you can see how far things moved. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I fix concrete steps that have pulled away from the house?

You can fill the gap with sealant to keep water out for a season or two, but it won't stop the movement. Steps that separate from the house are settling or heaving on their own base. Fixing it takes new support under the steps or a rebuild.

### Why are my concrete steps flaking?

That's spalling. Water and de-icing salt freeze in the top layer and pop it off. Badly finished concrete, or concrete salted in its first winter, is the most likely to do it. Resurfacing can help if the concrete underneath is still sound.

### Should I replace concrete steps with wood or composite?

Concrete holds up when it's built right. A wood or composite stair on footings is easier to rebuild later, takes a handrail without any fuss, and fits better against a deck or porch. Whichever you pick, it has to sit on a foundation that won't heave.
