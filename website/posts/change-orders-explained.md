A change order in remodeling is a short written agreement that changes the scope, price or schedule of a project after the original contract is signed. It describes what is changing, what it costs or saves, how it affects the timeline, and it is approved by both the homeowner and the contractor before the changed work goes ahead. Change orders are normal on almost every project. What causes trouble is changes agreed by a nod in the hallway and argued about when the final bill arrives.

## Why projects change

Even a carefully planned project changes. The most common reasons:

- **Hidden conditions.** Opening a wall or floor reveals rot, water damage, undersized framing or old wiring. This is especially common in the older houses found throughout Saratoga County's villages and cities.
- **Homeowner decisions.** You see the room taking shape and decide to add a pantry cabinet, move a light, or choose a different tile.
- **Product availability.** A fixture is discontinued or a material's lead time would delay the project.
- **Code or inspection requirements.** An inspector requires something that was not anticipated, such as added blocking, a different vent route or an upgraded circuit.

None of these is anyone's fault. They are simply part of remodeling, and a change order is the tool for handling them fairly.

## What a change order should include

A change order does not need to be long. A clear one fits on a single page and covers:

1. **The project and date**, and a reference to the original contract.
2. **What is changing**, described specifically enough that both sides picture the same thing. "Replace rotted subfloor under the tub area, approximately the size of the tub footprint, with new plywood" is useful. "Floor repair" is not.
3. **Why**, briefly, such as "hidden water damage found during demolition" or "homeowner requested upgrade."
4. **The price effect**: the added cost or the credit.
5. **The schedule effect**: how many days it adds, if any.
6. **The new contract total**, so everyone knows where the project stands.
7. **Signatures or written approval** from both parties before the work proceeds.

A change order should also say how payment for it fits the existing payment schedule, for example added to the next phase payment.

## The handshake problem

Verbal changes feel easier in the moment. The contractor says, "While we have the wall open, we could move that outlet," and you say, "Sure." Two weeks later the invoice includes a charge you did not expect, or the contractor assumed you understood it would cost extra while you assumed it was a small favor. Both people remember the conversation differently, and neither is lying.

A written change order prevents that. It takes a few minutes, and it turns an easy-to-forget conversation into a record both sides agreed to. It also protects you from small additions that quietly add up over the course of a project.

## Change orders and hidden conditions

Hidden conditions deserve special handling because they usually come up in the middle of a phase, often while a room is torn apart. A good process looks like this:

- The work stops at the affected area.
- The contractor shows you the problem, ideally in person, or with photos if you are away.
- The contractor explains the options, such as repair versus replace, and prices them.
- You choose, and the change is written up and approved.
- The work continues.

The estimate should already say this is how hidden conditions will be handled. ## Credits work the same way

Change orders are not only for added cost. If you decide to supply your own light fixtures, drop a section of tile, or choose a less expensive countertop, that is also a change, and it should produce a written credit against the contract. Keeping credits and additions on the same paperwork gives you an accurate running total.

## Keeping changes under control

A few habits keep change orders from taking over a project:

- **Make decisions early.** Choose fixtures, tile and finishes before work starts when you can. Most kitchen and bathroom changes come from decisions made late. Our [kitchen remodel guide for Saratoga Springs](/blog/kitchen-remodel-saratoga-springs-guide/) covers which decisions to make before demolition.
- **Ask for the price before saying yes.** Even for something small.
- **Keep a running list.** Know the current contract total after each change.
- **Group small changes.** If several minor changes come up in a week, they can go on one change order.

Changes also affect the payment schedule. Our post on [phased payments vs paying upfront](/blog/phased-payments-vs-paying-upfront/) explains how payments should stay tied to completed work as the scope evolves.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a contractor charge for changes that were not written down?

That is exactly the kind of dispute a change order is meant to prevent. Whatever your contract says, insisting on written change orders for every change in scope, price or schedule protects both sides from disagreements about what was agreed.

### Does a change order have to be on paper?

It needs to be in writing and approved by both parties. That can be a signed paper form or a written agreement by email, depending on what your contract allows. What matters is a clear record of the change, its price and its effect on the schedule, approved before the work.

### Are change orders a sign of a poorly planned project?

Not necessarily. Some changes, such as hidden rot, cannot be known before work starts. A project with many change orders driven by late decisions, however, is a sign that more planning up front would have helped.
