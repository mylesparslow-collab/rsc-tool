Under the New York State Residential Code, which is based on the International Residential Code, any flight of stairs with four or more risers needs a handrail on at least one side. It goes 34 to 38 inches above the front edge of the treads, runs the full length of the flight, and has to be shaped so a hand can wrap around it. That covers inside stairs, cellar stairs and outside steps the same way. In older Saratoga Springs houses, it's one of the things most often missing or loose.

## Counting risers

A riser is the vertical face of each step. Count them from the lower floor to the upper one. Three steps up to a landing is three risers and doesn't trigger the rule. Four does. A front stoop with four steps needs a rail just as much as the staircase to the second floor.

The count is per flight, not per staircase. If there's a landing partway up, each run between a floor and a landing gets counted on its own, so a short run of four steps up to a landing still needs a rail.

People don't expect outside steps to count, but that's where a rail does the most good. Wet, icy, snow-packed steps are exactly when somebody grabs for something, and in a Capital Region winter that's your front steps for months at a time. The steps themselves are covered in our post on [concrete and wood step repair](/blog/outdoor-steps-freeze-thaw/).

## A rail you can actually hold

A rail can be there and still fail the one time someone needs it. Height is 34 to 38 inches, measured straight up from the line that connects the nosings, the front edges of the treads. Measure at a few points; old rails sag, or got set at a different height at each end.

It should run without a break from above the bottom riser to above the top one, so a hand can slide the whole way without letting go. The classic shape is a round rail about 1-1/4 to 2 inches across. Wide flat boards and chunky decorative caps you can't get your fingers around don't meet the intent of the rule. Leave at least 1-1/2 inches between the rail and the wall so knuckles don't scrape. At the ends, the rail should return into the wall or die into a newel post, so a sleeve or a bag strap can't catch on an open end.

Then it has to be fastened to something solid. Brackets screwed only into drywall or old plaster will pull out under a person's full weight. On plaster walls we find the studs, or open the wall and add blocking, before a single bracket goes up.

## Handrails and guards aren't the same thing

The handrail is what you hold. The guard is the barrier that keeps you from going off an open side. On an open staircase with balusters they often share parts, but the rules are separate.

Under the residential code, a guard is required where a walking surface, including the open side of a stair, is more than 30 inches above the floor or ground below. On decks and porches the guard has to be at least 36 inches high, with openings between balusters small enough that a 4-inch sphere can't pass through. Along the sloped side of a stair, the height is measured from the tread nosings and the opening rule allows a little more. If you're planning a new stair or porch rail, your building department can confirm the exact numbers for your job. ## Old stairs in Saratoga Springs

The city has a lot of houses built long before anyone wrote a modern code, and their stairs are often handsome and a little off by today's rules. The pattern repeats. There's a turned newel and a full balustrade on the open side, and nothing at all on the wall side of a narrow, steep stair. Original rails sit at 30 or 32 inches, below the current range. The cellar stair has no rail, or a two-by-four nailed to the wall that quits a few steps short of the floor. A newel has worked loose over the decades and the whole balustrade sways when you lean on it. And the porch steps got rebuilt at some point with no rail at all.

Existing stairs generally aren't forced up to current code unless the work is being altered or permitted. Add a proper wall rail anyway. It's one of the best safety changes you can make in an older house, and it almost never means touching the original woodwork. On a staircase with historic value, we tighten and reinforce the original newel instead of replacing it; [staircase restoration](/blog/staircase-restoration-repair-or-replace/) explains how we choose between repair and replacement. If the work is on the outside of a house in one of the Saratoga Springs historic districts, ask the city first whether it needs Design Review Commission approval.

## When to add a rail the code doesn't require

The code is a minimum, and we'd go past it in a few places.

Put rails on both sides for anyone with a bad knee, a weak hand or shaky balance. They can then hold on with their stronger hand going up and coming down.

Short runs are classic trip spots. Two or three steps between the mudroom and the kitchen, or down into a sunken living room, catch people at night when they forget they're there.

Cellar stairs are often steep and dim, and people use them with an armload of laundry. Put a solid rail the full length no matter how many risers there are. More on those in [basement stair repair](/blog/basement-stair-repair/).

And if parents or grandparents visit, a sturdy rail at the front steps is often the first thing families add. Our [home safety checklist for elderly parents](/blog/home-safety-checklist-aging-parents/) lists other changes along the same lines.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do three steps need a handrail in New York?

Not under the residential code minimum, which starts at four risers. Short runs of two or three steps still cause plenty of falls, though, and a rail is a small job next to a broken wrist. Outside steps that ice over are the first ones we'd rail even when the rule doesn't apply.

### How high should a stair handrail be?

Between 34 and 38 inches, measured straight up from the line of the tread nosings to the top of the rail. Check it at several points along the stair, because older rails sometimes sag or were set at different heights at each end.

### Does an old staircase have to be brought up to code?

Generally no, unless the stair is being altered or the work needs a permit. Your town or city building department can tell you what applies to your project. Safety is a separate question. Fix a loose or missing rail either way.
